UCI HEART DISEASE · OBSERVATIONAL STUDY

What 920 heart examinations tell us

Among examinations with a recorded exercise-test response, angina was more common when the artery examination was positive. How much did the difference vary across centers, and could missing responses explain it?

Inside the study

Each record describes one examination at one of four medical centers. A positive result means the examination found coronary artery disease. These records describe people who reached a center for evaluation. They cannot tell us how common the disease was in the surrounding population.

THE FOUR CENTERS

How different were the four centers?

Before combining the records, we wanted to know whether the hospitals examined similar groups. If one center saw more people with disease, its share of the records would affect the overall percentage.

Of 920 examinations, 509 were positive. The overall 55.3% includes centers as different as Hungary (36.1%) and Switzerland (93.5%). Only eight Swiss examinations were negative. Referral patterns could help explain the difference, but the files do not record who was considered for referral.

DIAGNOSIS LABELS

What changes when we count a diagnosis differently?

We wanted to know whether the same counting rule meant the same thing in each file. A diagnosis code is the number stored in the examination record. The usual count includes labels 1 through 4 as positive and treats 0 as negative. What if we count only labels 2 through 4?

All 106 positive examinations in the processed file from Hungary carry label 1. Counting only labels 2 through 4 removes every one. The dataset’s original summary (heart-disease.names) lists 26, 28 and 15 Hungarian cases under labels 2, 3 and 4: 69 altogether. The discrepancy tells us about how records were stored; it cannot establish the severity of disease in Hungary.

EXERCISE SYMPTOMS

How much does angina tell us about the examination?

The exercise test records whether angina (chest pain or discomfort) occurred. A separate examination records whether the heart’s arteries showed disease. We started with the exercise-test result, then reversed the comparison and started with the artery examination.

Among the 865 examinations with a recorded exercise response, 282 of 337 with angina were positive (83.7%), compared with 192 of 528 without angina (36.4%). Now read the numbers the other way: of the 474 positive examinations with a known response, 192 (40.5%) had no angina recorded during exercise.

MISSING ANSWERS

Could the missing angina entries reverse the finding?

The exercise-test response is blank in 55 records, including 35 positive and 20 negative examinations. Because their diagnoses are known, we can find the full range of gaps that any assignment of these missing responses could produce.

Assigning every missing answer in the direction that most reduces the gap still leaves a 38.7 percentage-point difference. Assigning them in the opposite direction gives 50.2 points. These limits address the missing responses only.

MISSING READINGS

Whose cholesterol measurements are available?

Before comparing cholesterol levels, we asked who contributed a reading. A measurement that is missing more often in one group can change which people a comparison describes.

Cholesterol was available for 346 of 509 positives and 372 of 411 negatives. All 123 Swiss records had zero in this field, treated as an unavailable reading. The cholesterol distributions in the next section therefore represent three centers.

MEASUREMENT DISTRIBUTIONS

How much do the measurements overlap?

A difference between two middle values leaves an important question open. Do the groups occupy separate ranges, or do many people with opposite examination results have similar measurements? We compared the full distributions for each available measure.

The median (middle) age was 51 years among negatives and 57 among positives, yet their ages overlap. Blood pressure offers a different example: the middle reading was 130 mmHg in both groups. Looking across the distribution shows what a single middle value leaves out.

ADJUSTED COMPARISONS

How does age relate to a positive result among people of the same recorded sex and center?

The groups differ in age and in where they were examined. A separate model for each comparison lets us ask how the odds of a positive examination differ after accounting for selected characteristics.

For people of the same recorded sex and center, being ten years older was associated with 1.71 times the odds of a positive result (95% confidence interval 1.42–2.05). What can we conclude?

A positive result was more common when angina was recorded during exercise. Among the 865 examinations with a known response, the gap was 47.3 percentage points. Giving both groups the same mix of centers reduced it to 42.3 points. In a separate check, assigning all 55 missing responses in every possible way kept the unweighted gap across all 920 examinations between 38.7 and 50.2 points. Disease already present could contribute to both findings.

To study who develops disease later, we would need people who started without the diagnosis, dated measurements, treatment histories and follow-up. These records capture findings at examination.
